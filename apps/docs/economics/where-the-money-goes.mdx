Take a \$10M pool of receivables earning 18% a year gross.

| | |
|---|---|
| Pool income | \$1,800,000 |
| Paid to investors | −\$790,500 |
| PRISM fees | −\$136,000 |
| Credit losses at 4% | −\$400,000 |
| Originator's running costs | −\$200,000 |
| **Left for the originator** | **\$273,500** |

On \$1.5M of the originator's own money, that is an 18.2% return.

<Note>
  The 4% loss rate above is an assumption, not a measurement. It is the single
  largest swing factor in this table — every percentage point of loss is
  \$100,000. See [Disclosures](/disclosures).
</Note>

## How to read it

The three largest claims on pool income are, in order: the investors, the credit
losses, and the originator's own running costs. PRISM's fees are the smallest line.

What is left is the originator's profit. It is the residual — which is exactly why
the originator is the party that must hold first loss. It has the most to lose from
a bad book and the most to gain from a good one.

## Why the asset yield has to be high

Work backwards from this table and the constraint is obvious: the investor line is
largely fixed by what capital costs elsewhere, and the loss line is set by the
book. A pool earning 12% gross cannot fill in the same rows.

See [why the asset has to earn 15% or more](/economics/minimum-asset-yield).
